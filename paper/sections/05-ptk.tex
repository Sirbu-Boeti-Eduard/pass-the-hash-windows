\section{Pass-the-Key: the Kerberos Face of the Same Primitive}
\label{sec:ptk}

\subsection{Why the NT hash is also a Kerberos key}

Chapter~\ref{sec:pth} computed an NTLMv2 proof from sixteen bytes. This chapter asks a
KDC to issue a ticket from the same sixteen bytes, and it can because of one encryption
type rather than anything in Kerberos as a whole. For RC4-HMAC, etype \texttt{0x17}, the
client's long-term key is the NT hash itself. No derivation step separates the value read
out of LSASS from the value the KDC holds for the account. The hash is the key on both
sides, which makes the Kerberos variant a change of protocol rather than a change of
capability \cite{ms-kile}.

Every other etype in a domain breaks the identity. For AES128 and AES256, etype
\texttt{0x11} and \texttt{0x12}, the long-term key comes from the plaintext password
through a salted string-to-key function, built on PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA1 with a salt drawn from
the realm and the username \cite{rfc3962,ms-kile}. A hash cannot produce such a key. The salt is
public, the input is the password, and the sixteen bytes are not it. An NT hash is
therefore a Kerberos key for exactly one etype and inert for the other two.

That asymmetry governs the chapter. Pass-the-key works only while an RC4 path exists for
the account \cite{microsoft-2014-pth}. Where the path is absent the technique does not
become harder; it becomes impossible, and the alternatives are password recovery or a
different class of material. The same sentence explains why RC4 has outlived its
cryptographic reputation: it is the only etype whose key an attacker can hold without
learning the password. The literature carries the technique under two names, pass-the-key
after the material it consumes and overpass-the-hash after the operation it performs; they
describe the same exchange.

\subsection{Building an AS-REQ from a key}
\subsubsection{Pre-authentication with a key instead of a password}

An AS request built from a key is the ordinary AS request with one substitution.
Pre-authentication in Active Directory is PA-ENC-TIMESTAMP: a timestamp encrypted under
the client's long-term key and carried in the AS-REQ. The KDC decrypts it with the key it
holds for the account, checks the timestamp against the allowable skew, and reads a
successful decryption as proof of possession. When the long-term key is the NT hash, the
attacker computes the ciphertext the legitimate client computes, because that client also
keys the timestamp from a secret the KDC shares. Nothing separates the two at this step
\cite{ms-kile}.

Everything around the timestamp is untouched. The AS-REQ carries the same fields, the KDC
applies the same policy, and the AS-REP returns a TGT encrypted under the krbtgt key with
a session key wrapped under the client's long-term key. The TGT carries the user's PAC, so
authorization downstream reads the account's real group membership
\cite{ms-kerberos-overview}. No password appears in the exchange, and none is derived.

After the AS-REP the hash stops mattering. TGS requests are authenticated with the TGT
session key, service tickets are issued against the PAC, and every later step uses
material the KDC minted. The original secret is consumed once per ticket lifetime rather
than once per service, and the attacker's operating system ceases to be a constraint: a
TGT is a portable structure defined by RFC~4120, and any Kerberos implementation that
reads a keytab or credential cache can use it. Implementations that build such requests
from a key exist in both tool families this paper relies on
\cite{deply-mimikatz,fortra-impacket}.

\subsubsection{Encryption-type negotiation and the downgrade incentive}

Encryption types are negotiated, not imposed. The client lists the etypes it will accept
in the AS-REQ, the KDC selects one from the intersection of that list with what the
account permits, and the AS-REP reports the choice \cite{rfc4120,ms-kile}. A request
offering only \texttt{0x17} is honoured whenever the account still permits RC4 and refused
when it does not.

The interest in RC4 is consequently a policy question, not a cryptographic one. Downgrade
here is attractive rather than necessary: no stronger etype is being defeated, since an
AES exchange cannot be attempted from a hash at all. Offering \texttt{0x17} alone is the
only request that can be built, and whether the KDC accepts it depends on the account's
supported encryption types and the domain policy inherited by accounts that set none.

\subsection{What PtK buys over PtH}

Three gains follow that Chapter~\ref{sec:pth} cannot deliver. The first is reach into
estates that have removed NTLM and consider the problem closed. Restricting NTLM addresses
the protocol that carries the hash in a Type~3 message; it does nothing to a KDC that
still accepts RC4 pre-authentication for the same account. A domain with NTLM disabled and
RC4 untouched is not hardened against this technique, and the two settings sit on
different policy surfaces, argued from different premises.

The second is access to services that refuse NTLM. A service ticket satisfies any service
that accepts Kerberos and rejects NTLM, which includes hardened configurations and
services whose default posture is Kerberos only \cite{ms-kerberos-overview}. An NTLM
response computed from the hash has no route into such a service.

The third is independence from the attacker's platform and from the target's credential
store. The NTLM technique speaks the protocol from the attacking host or writes the hash
into LSASS; both keep the attacker inside the Windows authentication stack. A TGT
obtained once is a file, and the rest of the intrusion can be driven from any host with a
Kerberos client \cite{fortra-impacket}.

\subsection{Where PtK stops}

The technique stops where RC4 stops. If an account is restricted to \texttt{0x11} and
\texttt{0x12}, the AS-REQ cannot be built: the keys the KDC accepts are derived from the
plaintext, and the hash yields none of them. Removing RC4 from the domain's supported
encryption types, or from a trust along a referral path, does the same for every account
it covers. This is the negative result the proof of concept is designed to produce: the
same credential and the same request, failing at the KDC because the etype is gone rather
than because the implementation is wrong.

FAST and armouring are often listed as controls against this technique and do not belong in that
list. Armouring wraps the pre-authentication exchange in a key derived from an armour ticket,
which stops an attacker from capturing the encrypted timestamp and guessing passwords against it
offline \cite{ms-kile}. It does not stop an attacker who already holds the key: nothing is being
guessed, and the armour ticket itself is obtained in an initial AS exchange. FAST protects the
secrecy of the pre-authentication blob, not the possession of the long-term key, so the removal
still has to come from the etype. Protected Users is the exception that does both for the
accounts placed in it, whose members cannot authenticate with NTLM, cannot use RC4 or DES for
pre-authentication, and have no cached interactive logon \cite{ms-protected-users}.

Each carries an operational cost, and Chapter~\ref{sec:mitigation} treats them as protocol
hardening and measures what they break. Their role here is to mark the boundary.
Pass-the-key is pass-the-hash applied to a second protocol family, and it ends where the
key in hand stops being a key the protocol will accept.
